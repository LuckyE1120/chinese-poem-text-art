# Art direction and prompt

Use this after the poem text is settled. Replace bracketed fields with decisions grounded in that poem.

> Create a 3:4 Chinese typographic artwork from **[exact poem and author]**. The picture must be made from the poem's own readable Hanzi. The characters are the contours, textures, and motion of the scene, not captions on an illustration.
>
> Main motif: **[one concrete image from the poem]**, occupying roughly 60% of the composition. Build it only from **[the word that names or describes it]**. Supporting motifs: **[at most two or three]**, each built from its corresponding source words. Translate the poem's relations into position and direction: height becomes vertical placement, falling becomes a descending arc, flow becomes a continuous path, distance becomes smaller and lighter type.
>
> A reader must be able to follow one clear path and recover the entire poem exactly once in order. Source characters may repeat as visual texture; no other Hanzi may appear. Use one quiet color family, a small accent only when the poem supports it, strong large-to-small type contrast, and generous empty space. At thumbnail size, the main motif must still read. At full size, every character must be legible.
>
> Avoid non-text scenery, generic Chinese-style ornaments, decorative calligraphy that cannot be read, extra words, title blocks, signatures, seals, watermarks, Latin letters, and random glyphs.

## Decisions that improved the ten-poem series

- Start with the main image, not a uniform grid of small characters. A single moon, tree, boat, tower, or cup gives the eye a destination.
- Use a hierarchy: one large form; medium motifs for the action; small repeated characters as atmosphere. Dense characters everywhere flatten the image.
- Leave substantial unprinted space. Empty sky, river, or distance is part of the composition.
- Use the poem's nouns for the visible objects and its verbs for direction or movement.
- Keep the complete poem readable even if the scene uses repeated fragments. Do not claim that a beautiful but garbled image satisfies the text requirement.
